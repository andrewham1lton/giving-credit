Donated goods over $500, and over $5,000

The bigger a gift of goods, the more the IRS asks you to keep. The amounts are per gift, except where Form 8283 groups similar items.

Your deductionWhat to keep or file
Under $250A receipt from the charity with its name and address, the date and place, and a description. Where a receipt isn’t practical, your own written record of each item, its value and how you found it.
$250 to $500The charity’s written acknowledgment, in hand before you file.
Over $500The acknowledgment, plus Form 8283 Section A: when and how you acquired the item, and its cost.
Over $5,000The acknowledgment, a qualified appraisal and Form 8283 Section B.

Over $500

Once your goods come to more than $500, Form 8283 goes with your return. For an item or group of similar items over $500, Section A also asks when you acquired it, how (bought, gift, inheritance) and what it cost you. See what Section A asks for.

Clothing and household items must be in good used condition or better. A single item over $500 that isn’t can still count, but only with a qualified appraisal and Section B.

Over $5,000

  • A qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser. Its valuation date can be no earlier than 60 days before the gift, and you need it by your return’s due date, including extensions.
  • Form 8283 Section B, the appraisal summary. The charity completes and signs its part.
  • Similar items count together. Three paintings at $2,000 each are a $6,000 group.
  • Publicly traded stock doesn’t need an appraisal; Section A covers it at any amount.

The appraisal is a separate document from Form 8283. The form summarizes it; it doesn’t replace it.

Where Giving Credit fits

Giving Credit asks for the Form 8283 details on any item over $500 and flags items over $5,000 as needing a qualified appraisal. It records goods, money and mileage; it doesn’t record stock.

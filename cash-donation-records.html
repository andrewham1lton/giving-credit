Keeping records of money you give

“Cash” here means any money gift: cash, check, card, bank transfer or payroll deduction. Whatever the amount, the IRS asks you to keep a record of it.

What counts as a record

  • A bank record that shows the charity’s name, the date and the amount: a canceled check, a bank or card statement.
  • A receipt, letter or email from the charity with its name, the date and the amount.
  • For payroll giving, a pay stub or W-2 showing the date and amount, plus the pledge card from the charity.

A note in your own diary isn’t enough for a money gift, even a small one.

$250 or more

A single gift of $250 or more also needs a written acknowledgment from the charity, in hand before you file. Separate gifts aren’t added together. See what the letter must say.

If you take the standard deduction, from 2026

From tax year 2026, people who don’t itemize can deduct cash gifts of up to $1,000, or $2,000 on a joint return. The limit counts:

  • money gifts made during that year only, with no carryover from earlier years
  • to public charities and similar organizations
  • but not gifts to donor-advised funds or to supporting organizations

Donated goods don’t count toward it. The same records are still needed for every gift. If you itemize, a different rule applies from 2026: a floor of 0.5% of your adjusted gross income.

A simple habit

Each time you give, write down the date, the charity, the amount and how you paid, and save the email receipt to one folder. In January, check that every gift of $250 or more has its letter.

Where Giving Credit fits

Giving Credit records each money gift by amount, charity, date and how you paid, reads a photographed receipt on your iPhone for you to check, and shows each gift’s proof status. If you say you take the standard deduction, it tracks your gifts against the $1,000 or $2,000 limit.
